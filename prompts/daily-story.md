# Daily story task (read fully, then do it)

You write one new sleep story for the YouTube channel Fernwick Nights and publish it by committing a JSON file.

**Since 2026-10-15 every story is a Greek myth about a figure most listeners have not met yet** (Khalid, 2026-10-15: make them interesting; 2026-10-16: make them gripping so listeners get hooked). Each night: one figure most listeners have never heard of, a strong hook, real substance from the ancient sources, told calmly and slowly for sleep by a different wise teller. The channel name, logo, intro and outro stay the same. Fernwick-town tales are retired. Greek myth is told as old stories, never as worship or belief.

## Steps
1. Read INSTRUCTIONS.md (the channel rules on one page), then world/greek_figures.csv (every figure already used; never repeat one), world/myth_ideas.md (a starter list of figures, with notes) and the last ~14 rows of world/story_log.csv. Skim the recent files in published/ so you do not repeat a premise, teller, opening image or ending image.
2. Choose tonight's figure yourself (see "Choosing the figure" below). If a multi-part story is unfinished in the log or published/, continue it first.
3. Write the story as described in the rules below.
4. Save it as queue/YYYY-MM-DD.json for the date you are running on (UTC date is fine).
5. Add a row to world/greek_figures.csv (date, figure, story_title, sources, teller). Mark the figure as used in world/myth_ideas.md if it is on that list, and add any good new ideas you came across.
6. Run `python scripts/qc.py queue/YYYY-MM-DD.json`. If it fails, fix the story and run it again until it passes (up to 3 tries; if it still fails, do not commit; stop and explain).
7. Commit everything to the main branch with the message `Story YYYY-MM-DD: <title>`. Pushing the file in queue/ starts the video build and a private YouTube upload automatically. Do not change anything else.

## Choosing the figure
- **Fresh to the listener.** Skip the household names (Zeus, Hera, Athena, Apollo, Aphrodite, Hercules/Heracles, Achilles, Odysseus, Perseus, Medusa, Persephone, Pandora, Icarus, Narcissus, Pygmalion, Orpheus, Prometheus). They may appear as side characters, and it is a good hook to link tonight's figure to one of them ("You know the hero who carried the sky for an afternoon. Tonight is about the three sisters whose apples he came for.").
- **Interesting.** The figure needs one striking, specific thing a stranger will want to hear: a strange gift, a transformation, a promise, a loyal act, a clever trick, an odd job among the gods, a constellation or a word in English that comes from them (halcyon days, the halcyon bird; Morpheus and morphine; the echo; the arachnid).
- **Calm enough to tell at bedtime.** Prefer myths of wonder, transformation, craft, kindness, loyalty, stars, sea, seasons, sleep and night. Skip myths built around sexual assault, abduction, cruelty to children, cannibalism or gore, even when famous. Sorrow is allowed when told gently and at a distance (see rule 2).
- **Vary** the kind of figure (nymph, mortal, Titan, minor god, monster-turned-gentle, constellation, river, wind) and the part of the Greek world (islands, mountains, rivers, seas, the far west and the far north of the myths). Do not use the same kind of figure two nights in a row.
- Cozy-spooky season, 17 to 31 October: about every other night may lean toward night and shadow, told gently: Nyx and her children, Hypnos and the Oneiroi, the river Lethe, Asteria, Hecate's torchlit crossroads told as legend, the ferryman's quiet river. Nothing truly frightening; no ghosts with intent, no monsters on the hunt.

## Rules
1. **Retell, never copy.** Use the ancient sources (Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns, Homer, Pindar, Apollodorus' Library, Ovid's Metamorphoses, Hyginus, Pausanias, Apollonius of Rhodes, Antoninus Liberalis, Nonnus and similar), which are public domain. Write an original telling in our own words; never follow the wording or structure of a modern translation, retelling, novel, film or game, since those can be copyrighted. Be accurate: names, parents, places and events should match the sources. Where the ancient writers disagree, the teller can say so ("Ovid says it was a quail. Others say she fell like a star into the sea."); that is good substance. Do not invent major plot events and present them as the myth; small sensory details and the frame are yours. Fill myth_sources with the ancient sources you followed and source_note with one sentence on why it is copyright-safe.
2. **Calm, but with something behind it.** A myth has events: a wish, a mistake, a jealous god, a long wait, a loss. Tell them, because that is what makes the story interesting, but tell them softly and at a distance, the way a grandmother tells an old sad tale: no gore, no battle detail, no cruelty dwelt on, no jump or loud moment, no cliffhanger. Hard moments come early or in the middle, are told in a sentence or two and are softened (a death becomes "and his long day was over", a punishment becomes a change of shape). The last third is always calm: the transformation complete, the stars in place, the sea quiet, the figure at rest.
3. **Myth, not worship.** Gods and goddesses appear as characters in old stories. No prayer, worship, sacrifice, altars, temples as places of worship, priests or priestesses, rituals, or anything framed as true belief. The oracle, if it appears, is a voice in a story, briefly. No content from living religions.
4. **Concrete and specific, every scene** (Khalid, 2026-10-08): each scene gives the listener a sound, a smell (or taste), a texture and a temperature, plus precise sights (colors, shapes, light). The Greek world is rich in this: thyme and salt on the wind, warm stone, olive wood smoke, cicadas, bronze, figs, the wine-dark sea, goats' bells, white dust on a road. Name things exactly: "three apples, gold as late sun, one with a leaf still on its stem", not "some apples".
5. Write like a human storyteller: plain words, varied sentence openings, occasional gentle humor. **Never say "lesser known", "lesser-known", "little-known", "little known" or "obscure"** (Khalid, 2026-10-16), not in the script, titles, description, tags or any other field; show why the figure is worth meeting instead of labeling them. Do not use: tapestry, symphony, embrace, testament, nestled, whisper of, a sense of, as if the world itself, little did, in the heart of, bustling, delve, journey of, magical.
6. **A wise teller tells every story** (Khalid, 2026-10-08). Each night a different invented teller tells the myth to someone: an old shepherd on a hillside above the sea, a woman who mends nets on a harbor wall, a beekeeper on a mountain of thyme, a lighthouse keeper on a far cape, a grandmother by an olive-wood fire, a ferryman crossing between two islands, a retired schoolteacher who knows every star. Tellers are invented, never real people; they can live in Greece today, long ago, or anywhere the old stories travel. No teller twice within 7 days (check the teller column of world/story_log.csv). The teller speaks in the first person to the listener, gently addressing them as "you" ("Sit here, the stone is still warm. I will tell you why the sunflowers turn."), adds a few short asides of their own (what they think, where they first heard it, a dry little joke), and the myth itself is told in plain present-tense narration. Keep the frame light: a few lines at the start, a few touches in the middle, a little at the end. No breathing or relaxation instructions and no "sleep well" in the story itself. At most three short lines of dialogue from the myth's characters.
7. Avoid words the checker blocks as alarming or religious even in passing (for example danger, dead*, died, gun*, attack, panic, scream, blood, crash, monster, knife, suddenly, holy, angel, pray*, church, saint, bible, temple, altar, sacrifice, priest). Find a gentler word; do not work around the check.
8. Mark pauses with [pause] (2 s) and [long pause] (4 s) on their own lines between paragraphs, more toward the end.
9. **Long myths become parts** (Khalid, 2026-10-07): if a myth cannot be told well in one video (about 2,000 words), split it into parts on consecutive days. Put "(Part 1 of 3)" in story_title and in every title option, decide the number of parts up front and record the plan in new_world_facts, and write each part as a complete, calm story that ends settled for sleep (never a cliffhanger). Open parts 2 and later with one soft sentence recalling the last part. Parts of one story share one row per part in world/greek_figures.csv with the same figure.

## Keep them listening (Khalid, 2026-10-16)
Khalid wants listeners hooked so they stay with the story. Calm does not mean flat: the story pulls the listener forward gently until the payoff, and only then lets them go.
- **Open a loop in the first lines.** The hook is a real question, a secret, a strange image or a surprising true fact that a stranger cannot leave unanswered ("There is a bird that can make the sea lie still for fourteen days in the middle of winter. It used to be a woman."). Not a mood, not scenery. Put it in open_question.
- **Make the listener care.** By the end of the context they know what the figure wants or fears, and what stands in the way. A want and an obstacle are what make a myth a story.
- **A gentle mystery runs through the middle.** Plant one or two small things early (an odd gift, a warning, a promise, a thing that does not add up) and pay them off later. Let the teller hold a detail back: "I will tell you what was in the jar, but not yet."
- **Something new in every chapter.** Each chapter gives a small reveal, a turn or a surprising detail from the sources (how the versions differ, a word we still use, a star you can find tonight). End each chapter on a soft curiosity, never a cliffhanger: a question to wonder about, not a fear.
- **A satisfying payoff, then rest.** The open question is answered clearly, ideally with one small surprise, before the last third (put it in payoff). After that nothing new is introduced; the story winds down with the figure at rest.
- **Choose the version and figure with the best story.** When picking a figure, prefer the one with the strangest true detail and the clearest want, and lead with that detail.
- **Titles and the Short sell the question.** Titles hint at the mystery without giving it away ("Why the Kingfisher Can Calm the Winter Sea | Alcyone"); the short_excerpt is the moment the question is posed.

## Structure of the script (about 1,750 to 2,150 words in total; at ~110 words a minute that is 16 to 20 minutes)
Khalid (2026-10-08): stories must open with a hook that keeps listeners with us before they drift off, give real context, and be rich in description and substance. Each story has a clear arc that eases down over its last third.

Chapters: put `[[CHAPTER Name]]` on its own line where each part begins (they become YouTube chapters). The script starts with `[[CHAPTER Welcome]]`, the first telling has 4 to 6 chapters with short, gentle names (Who She Was, The Garden at the Edge of the World, Gold in the Branches), and the second telling starts with `[[CHAPTER Once More, Slowly]]`. The build adds a final "Quiet ambience to stay asleep" chapter by itself.

A. Intro (one paragraph, about 40 to 50 words): starts exactly "Welcome to Fernwick Nights." Then says tonight's story title, that it comes from the Greek myths, and the teller ("Tonight's story is The Girl Who Watched the Sun, from the old Greek myths, told by Eleni, who keeps bees on the slopes above the sea."). Word it freshly each night so the intro never matches an earlier one, and ends with one soft "get comfortable" line. Then `[long pause]`.
B. Hook (about 100 to 150 words): the teller greets the listener in a line or two (where they are sitting together), then the hook: the striking question or fact about tonight's figure (why does a flower follow the sun all day? who was the boy the Moon would not let grow old? what did the nymph give the shipwrecked sailor?). Name the figure early. Never open with the date or the weather. Answer the question by the middle of the story at the latest.
C. Context (about 100 to 150 words): who the figure is (parents, home, what they do), where in the Greek world it happens, roughly when in the myths (before the Titans fell, in the age of heroes...), and how they touch a myth the listener may already know. A new listener should be able to follow everything after this.
D. Background and substance (2 or 3 paragraphs, woven in): what the ancient writers say, where the story was told, how versions differ, what of it survives today (a constellation, a flower, a word, a place name). Told as storytelling, not a lecture, and accurate.
E. The myth (about 1,000 to 1,200 words in 16 to 22 short paragraphs): the story told scene by scene, rich in sensory detail (rule 4), with one or two moments of gentle humor or character from the teller. Any hard moment comes early or mid-story and is told softly (rule 2). The last third slows: shorter sentences, more [long pause] markers, the figure at rest (a flower closing, a star settling, a sea gone still), and the teller and listener settling too.
F. A line `[[SECOND]]` on its own, then `[[CHAPTER Once More, Slowly]]` and the second telling (about 120 to 200 words): the same myth retold more simply and slowly, opening with a one-line echo of the hook.
G. Outro (one or two sentences from the teller, then a `[long pause]`): a soft goodbye that hands the listener over to the quiet ("And that is why the sunflower turns. The fire is low now, and you can stay as long as you like."). Final line exactly: "Good night." The build then plays a few minutes of ambience alone.

Voice: pick the voice that suits tonight's teller from this list (all free edge-tts voices) and put it in the "voice" field: en-US-AvaNeural (warm, soft female), en-US-AndrewNeural (warm, low male), en-GB-SoniaNeural (calm, dry female, British), en-GB-RyanNeural (gentle male, British), en-US-EmmaNeural (bright, kind female), en-US-BrianNeural (relaxed male), en-IE-EmilyNeural (soft female, Irish), en-IE-ConnorNeural (soft male, Irish). Prefer Ava or Andrew unless the teller clearly calls for another. Greek names must be easy for the voice: write them the common English way (Hesperides, Alcyone, Philoctetes) and avoid accent marks.

Before saving, reread the first three paragraphs and ask: would a stranger who has never heard of this figure need to hear what happens next, and do they know who, where and why? Then reread each chapter's last lines: does each one leave a small reason to keep listening, until the payoff? If not, rewrite them.

## JSON file keys (copy published/2026-10-08.json for the exact shape of the shared fields)
- story_title: the story's own name, gentle and intriguing (The Girl Who Watched the Sun).
- series: always "Greek Myths for Sleep".
- myth_figure: tonight's main figure, the usual English spelling (Clytie). It must appear in every title option and must not already be in world/greek_figures.csv under a different story.
- myth_sources: list of the ancient sources followed (e.g. ["Ovid, Metamorphoses 4", "Hyginus, Fabulae 14"]).
- place: where the myth happens (e.g. "The Garden of the Hesperides", "Delos", "Mount Latmos").
- character: same as myth_figure.
- season, ambience (one word: rain, wind, ocean, harbor or stove; match the teller's setting), summary (2 sentences for the log).
- teller ({"name": "...", "who": "a few words: who they are and where"}), listener (who the teller is telling it to and where, e.g. "a hiker resting on the warm stone wall of the shepherd's fold at dusk"), voice (from the voice list above).
- open_question (one sentence: the question the hook plants that the listener carries through the story, e.g. "Why would a girl who loved the Sun end up rooted in the ground?").
- payoff (one sentence: how that question is answered, in words that also appear in the first telling, between the middle and the start of the last third).
- hook (one sentence: the question or striking fact the story opens with; most of its words must appear in the first 150 words of the script), context (one or two sentences: who the figure is, where, and how they connect to the better-known myths).
- source_tradition (e.g. "Ancient Greece (Ovid and Hyginus; set in Persia and Rhodes)"), source_note (one sentence on why it is copyright-safe).
- title_options (3 titles, each under 100 characters, that are easy to find in search: the figure's name, a hint of what is strange about them, and a search phrase, e.g. "Clytie, the Girl Who Turned Into a Sunflower | Greek Mythology Sleep Story" or "The Forgotten Nymph Who Followed the Sun | Clytie | Greek Myths for Sleep"; rotate the search phrases: Greek Mythology Sleep Story, Greek Myths for Sleep, Bedtime Myth for Adults, Greek Mythology Bedtime Story).
- thumbnail_text (2 to 4 words, usually the figure's name and a hint: "Clytie, the Sun Watcher").
- description_summary (1 sentence), description_setting (2 to 3 sentences: who the figure is and where the myth happens), hashtags (3, e.g. greekmythology, sleepstory, bedtimestoriesforadults), tags (10, include the figure's name, "greek mythology", "greek myths for sleep", "sleep story").
- light (the one flickering light source in the picture: {"type": "candle|lantern|fire|window|lamp", "x": 0.0-1.0, "y": 0.0-1.0} as fractions of the picture, pointing at where that light sits; use candle, lantern or fire only if the scene really has one, else window or lamp).
- soundscape (2 to 4 background sound layers that match the scene, from: room (indoor hum), crackle (fire or stove), clock (soft ticking), rain, drips (drops on glass), wind, ocean (far-off waves), lapping (small harbor waves), stream, crickets (warm nights only), pines (wind in pine trees); e.g. a shepherd's fire on a hillside ["wind", "crackle", "crickets"], a harbor wall at night ["lapping", "ocean", "wind"]; the sound plays under the whole story, rises softly when it ends and carries on alone for 10 minutes).
- motion (optional list of moving effects, from: embers, smoke, rain, snow, dust, zoom; leave it out and the build picks them).
- image_brief (one dark, warm, specific scene from tonight's myth, painted fresh by the build, so describe it well: the landscape or room in the ancient Greek world, the one warm light, two or three objects from the story, the sky or sea; no faces, no text, no statues of gods, no temples, no religious symbols; figures only small or from behind).
- short_excerpt (70 to 110 consecutive words copied from the story, the most intriguing, no pause markers; it becomes the Short, so it should make a stranger curious about the figure).
- new_world_facts (list: notes for later nights, e.g. a related figure worth its own story, or a multi-part plan), script.
